Before the heatmap: three questions for a clearer analysis

October 28, 2026 · Example post

Sample post. This article demonstrates the blog layout and writing workflow. It is illustrative content, not a report of research results.

A heatmap can be a useful way to explore an analysis. Before choosing its colors or arranging its rows, it helps to write down what the figure should let someone understand.

Here are three questions worth putting at the top of an analysis notebook.

1. What is the question?

“Explore the data” is a reasonable starting point, but a difficult stopping rule. A more specific question makes it easier to decide which cells, samples, and measurements belong in the comparison.

For a hypothetical spatial analysis, the question might be:

How do the measured expression patterns differ between the tumor core and the infiltrating edge in this dataset?

This is a question, not a claim about the answer. Keeping the two separate makes the next steps easier to explain.

2. What does each column represent?

A column might represent a cell, a spatial spot, a patient, or an average across a group. Those choices change what a reader can infer from the figure.

Decision Example to document
Unit shown A cell, spot, sample, or group average
Grouping Anatomical region or annotated cell state
Transformation The normalization and scaling used
Ordering A clustering rule or a predefined biological order

The purpose is to make the figure interpretable before its appearance becomes the focus.

3. Could someone reconstruct the comparison?

A useful notebook records the choices that are easy to forget: the input file, the filtering step, the metadata column, and the output location.

Even a small configuration block can make the intent visible:

# Illustrative configuration, not a complete analysis pipeline.
comparison = {
    "group_by": "anatomical_region",
    "groups": ["core", "edge"],
    "unit": "sample",
    "output": "figures/core_edge_heatmap.png",
}

The configuration does not establish that the comparison is valid. It gives the analyst a concise place to state what is being compared and where the result will go.

A small habit

Before making the figure, write one sentence for the question, one for the comparison, and one for the limits of interpretation. Keep them beside the code.

When the analysis is revisited, those three sentences can be more useful than another round of adjustments to the color palette.
